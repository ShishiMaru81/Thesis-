Fine-tuning is widely used to adapt large language models to specialised domains, and is typically evaluated on task accuracy alone. We show that this evaluation misses a failure mode that fine-tuning creates.

We built a bilingual Bengali–English question-answering system for irrigation advisory, targeting runtime-conditioned queries whose answers depend on live evapotranspiration, rainfall, crop stage and field size, and therefore cannot be retrieved from any document. Four models (Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-NeMo-12B, Phi-3.5-mini, Phi-4-mini) were fine-tuned with three seeds each on a leakage-audited corpus derived from BARC crop calendars, and evaluated on a hardened 422-item test set (362 items scored) containing adversarial misspellings, unit ambiguity and incomplete queries. Five annotators rated 800 model outputs, and all hypotheses were tested under an analysis plan fixed before any significance test was computed.

Fine-tuning taught the models the system's conventions, which no base model possessed. All four scored 0.000 F1 on the crop-coefficient table and on the tiered effective-rainfall rule before training, and 0.542–0.761 and 0.930–0.999 respectively after. Accuracy within ±0.1 mm on single-crop queries rose from 0.0–3.4% to 38.8–69.3%, a gain that held in every model and seed.

The same training produced no clarification behaviour in any of twelve training runs. When a required parameter was absent, every fine-tuned model supplied a value the user never gave and computed with it, while refusing out-of-scope questions correctly; abstention appeared in the training corpus and clarification did not. Base models also invented values, but often visibly: 13.2–66.2% of their responses to incomplete queries ran to the token limit, and 15–42% of their invented answers said a value had been assumed. After fine-tuning, 98.5–100% were complete, unhedged, confident errors in every model, and annotators judged none of the evaluated fine-tuned models' responses to incomplete queries fit to give a farmer.

Adding 300 clarification examples raised clarification that names the missing parameter to 29.4–38.2% and cut invented values from 100% to 50.0–66.2%, with no detectable loss of numeric accuracy. Bengali trailed English on factual text in all four models (6.4–12.8 chrF++ points), significantly in three; differences in numeric accuracy and in human ratings between the languages were not significant.
